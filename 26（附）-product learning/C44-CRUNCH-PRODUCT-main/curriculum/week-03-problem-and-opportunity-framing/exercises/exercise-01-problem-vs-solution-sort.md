# Exercise 1 — Sort Problems from Solutions

**Goal:** Get fast and reliable at spotting the difference between a problem, a symptom, and a solution wearing a problem's clothes — the single most common early mistake this week's lectures warn about.

**Estimated time:** 1 hour.

## Setup

No database needed. Create a file `sort.md` in your portfolio. For each of the 15 statements below, write:

1. Your classification: **Problem**, **Symptom**, or **Solution**.
2. One sentence of reasoning.
3. If you classified it as a Symptom or a Solution, rewrite it as a proper problem statement (who/what/why/impact — you don't need hard numbers for this exercise, a plausible placeholder is fine, but flag it as a placeholder).

## The 15 statements

All are things a real stakeholder might say out loud at Loopline. Some are trickier than they look — that's the point.

1. "We need a dark mode toggle in settings."
2. "37% of trial teams never create a second task after their first session."
3. "Add a Slack integration so people get notified there."
4. "New teams keep asking support how to invite their whole team at once."
5. "Users on mobile can't attach files to a task."
6. "We should just copy what Asana does for their onboarding flow."
7. "Teams whose CSV import fails convert to paid at a quarter of the rate of teams whose import succeeds."
8. "Enterprise prospects keep asking for SSO before they'll sign."
9. "Search is slow."
10. "We should build an AI assistant that auto-writes task descriptions."
11. "Free-tier teams that hit the 3-project limit almost never upgrade — they just create a second free account instead."
12. "Customers are unhappy with support response times."
13. "Let's redesign the task detail page, it looks dated."
14. "New teams who don't complete the guided setup checklist have half the week-2 retention of teams who do — but we don't know if the checklist causes that or just correlates with more engaged teams."
15. "Give admins a bulk user-import CSV like the one Slack has."

## Notes on the trickier ones

- A statement can be **both** a solution *and* hint at a real problem underneath — your job is to name the problem it's hinting at, not just say "solution, reject."
- A statement with a real number in it (like #2, #7, #11) is evidence of a symptom, not automatically a well-formed problem statement — check whether it also names *who* and *why*, per Lecture 1's checklist.
- #14 is deliberately honest about a limitation (correlation vs. causation) — that's a strong trait in a real problem statement, not a weakness. Notice it, don't "fix" it by pretending the causation is proven.
- Not every statement needs a heroic root-cause investigation for this exercise — a plausible, stated root-cause hypothesis is enough; you're not expected to run a real Five Whys against a fictional stakeholder.

## Done when…

- [ ] All 15 statements are classified with one sentence of reasoning each.
- [ ] Every statement classified as Symptom or Solution has a rewritten problem statement attached.
- [ ] At least 3 of your rewrites explicitly name a segment ("who") more specific than "users" or "customers."
- [ ] You can explain, for #6 and #10, what's specifically wrong with "copy a competitor" and "add AI" as stated — both are extremely common real-world versions of a solution-shaped statement.

## Stretch

Pick the two statements you found hardest to classify. Write 3–4 sentences on *why* they were hard — usually the honest answer is "there isn't enough information yet to tell," which is itself a useful thing to notice and say out loud to a stakeholder, rather than guessing.
